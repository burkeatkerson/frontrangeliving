The Railroad Museum understands its audience perfectly: it is not a
building with artifacts, it is a train yard you are allowed into.
A hundred locomotives and cars sit in long rows under North Table
Mountain — narrow-gauge steam from the mining lines, the great
rotary snowplows, gallery after gallery of cabooses with their
steps down and doors open — and a kid can spend an hour just
climbing before noticing the exhibits exist.

The rhythm of the good visit: check the **operating calendar**
before choosing the Saturday, because a ride day (steam some
weekends, diesel others; $2 a kid behind real equipment around the
demonstration loop) transforms the whole outing. Arrive at opening,
do the **roundhouse** first — an actual working restoration shop
where volunteers rebuild locomotives behind a viewing rail, with the
armstrong **turntable** out front usually spinning for
demonstrations — then ride, then let the **G-scale garden railway's**
kid-triggered buttons close the show. The picnic grove among the
rolling stock makes the packed lunch scenic; Golden's
[Clear Creek and ice cream](/answers/free-things-kids-front-range)
are eight minutes away for the afterparty.

The ticketed spectaculars — **Day Out With Thomas** in spring,
the **Polar Express** in winter (pajamas, cocoa, Santa boarding at
"the North Pole") — are the 3-to-7 bullseye and book out absurdly
early; the Polar Express is a September purchase for a December
ride. Membership (~$70–90 family) pays off fast for a genuinely
train-phase household and includes event presales, which is the
actual reason to buy it.

Where it sits in the larger obsession: this is the anchor of
[the whole train ladder](/answers/train-obsessed-kid-colorado) — the
cheap, close, repeatable rung that makes the Georgetown Loop and
the cog make sense later. Pair it with [Dinosaur Ridge](/answers/dinosaurs-with-kids-colorado)
ten minutes south when siblings' obsessions diverge.
