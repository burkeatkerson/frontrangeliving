Every bad Colorado pro forma we have been sent shares the same three
mistakes: a rent number from a listing site rather than a lease, an insurance
line copied from wherever the buyer lives now, and a tax line that ignores the
metro district. This is the worksheet that fixes all three, in the order we fill
it in.

## The nine lines

| Line | What goes in it | Front Range default, 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Rent | A signed lease, or three comparable *leased* units within a mile | Denver 3-bed house ~$2,900–3,100; Springs ~$2,100–2,300; Pueblo ~$1,600–1,800 |
| 2. Vacancy | Weeks empty between tenants plus non-payment | 5% metro, 6–7% Springs/Pueblo, 8% single-employer towns |
| 3. Management | What a licensed manager actually charges here | 8–10% of collected rent, plus a half- to full-month leasing fee |
| 4. Maintenance | Repairs under about $1,500 | 6–8% of rent; 10% on anything built before 1960 |
| 5. Capital reserve | Roof, furnace, sewer line, water heater | $150–250/month on a house; roofs here are a hail question, not an age question |
| 6. Property tax | Actual value × assessment rate × mill levy | See [the property-tax guide](/investing/property-tax-for-colorado-investors); ranges from ~0.45% to ~1.2% of value |
| 7. Insurance | A quote for *this* address, landlord policy | $2,400–4,000/yr on a metro house; more in the foothills and the hail belt |
| 8. HOA / metro district | Dues, plus debt-service mills on new builds | $0 on an old Denver ranch; $250–600/month on a Parker townhome; 40–70 extra mills in a new-build metro district |
| 9. Debt service | Principal and interest at the investor rate | ~7.25% at 25% down in August 2026 |

The sum of lines 2 through 8 is your operating expense ratio. On the Front
Range it runs **38–45% of rent on a house and 45–55% on a condo**, once the
HOA is in. If a pro forma shows you 30%, it is leaving out a line.

## Where each number comes from

**Rent.** Zillow and Rentometer estimates skew high here because they blend
new Class A apartments into single-family comps. Use leases: ask the listing
agent for the current lease on anything sold tenanted, and ask two property
managers in the submarket what they *signed* last month, not what they listed.
Metro Denver average apartment rent sat at about $1,758 in Q2 2026, down
roughly 4.7% year on year, and single-family rents followed it down by less.

**Vacancy.** The metro apartment vacancy rate fell to 5.74% in Q2 2026 from
6.84% in Q1, after 2025 delivered more apartments than any year on record.
Single-family vacancy runs lower than apartment vacancy in every Front Range
submarket, but a house sits empty longer between tenants than a unit in a
leased-up building, so 5% is honest.

**Management.** Out-of-state owners need a manager, full stop — Colorado's
2024–2026 landlord-tenant changes (see [the statewide rules
page](/investing/colorado-landlord-law-2026)) turned self-management from
elsewhere into a liability. Budget 8–10% plus leasing.

**Taxes.** The single most mis-underwritten line. Colorado's residential
assessment rate is 6.8% for local government and 7.05% for schools in tax
year 2026, applied after a 10% reduction of the first $700,000 of actual
value. Multiply by a mill levy that ranges from the high 60s in parts of
Colorado Springs to 140-plus in a Douglas County metro district.

**Insurance.** Colorado's average homeowners premium reached about $6,630 in
2026, the fourth highest in the country, and hail — not wildfire — accounts for
roughly half of what Front Range owners pay. Get a bound quote before you
waive inspection, and ask specifically about the wind/hail deductible, which
is increasingly written as 1–2% of dwelling coverage rather than a flat $1,000.

## Three worked cases

We keep three standing examples current, because the answer changes by
submarket more than it changes by year:

- [A 1954 brick ranch in Denver](/investing/rent-vs-carry-denver-brick-ranch)
  — the appreciation bet that costs you money every month.
- [A military rental in southeast Colorado Springs](/investing/rent-vs-carry-colorado-springs-military-rental)

- [A house in Pueblo](/investing/rent-vs-carry-pueblo) — the closest thing to
  day-one cash flow the Front Range offers.

## The question the worksheet actually answers

At 25% down and an investor rate above 7%, the Front Range does not cash-flow
with a manager on day one, anywhere, on a plain house. That is not a reason
not to buy; it is the reason to be clear about what you are buying. In Denver
and Boulder County you are buying ten years of appreciation and rent growth
and paying a monthly premium for it. In Pueblo and southeast Colorado Springs
you are buying break-even within two or three years of rent growth, and
accepting thinner resale demand for it. The [cash-flow-versus-appreciation
map](/investing/cash-flow-vs-appreciation-front-range) lays out where each
submarket sits.
