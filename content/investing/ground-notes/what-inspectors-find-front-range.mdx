Front Range houses are, on the whole, well built — brick, on footings, in a
dry climate that is kind to wood. They also sit on clay, on clay sewer pipe,
over uranium-bearing granite, under a hail belt, and behind seventy years of
electrical code. The inspection report on a 1958 ranch in Lakewood and the
report on a 1962 ranch in Colorado Springs say the same six things, and the
investor's job is to know which are offer-price items and which are
walk-away items.

## 1. Expansive soils

Much of the Front Range piedmont is underlain by bentonite-bearing clays
(the Pierre Shale and Denver formations, among others) that swell when they
take on water and shrink when they dry. The Colorado Geological Survey has
mapped swelling-soil hazard across the whole corridor; the worst zones run
along the hogback from Golden through Ken Caryl to Roxborough, through
parts of Aurora, Thornton and Northglenn, and across the Springs' east side
and Pueblo.

**What you see:** diagonal cracks from window corners, doors that stick
seasonally, a garage slab heaved an inch, a basement floor that is not flat,
a gap between the floor and the bottom of a basement partition wall (good:
the wall was built floating for this reason).

**What it costs:** nothing, if the movement is old and stable — most
post-war houses have moved and stopped. A structural engineer's letter
($400–800) if it is not obviously stable. Pier and beam underpinning
($15,000–60,000) if it is active. Grading and gutter extensions ($1,000–
3,000) to keep water away from the foundation are the real maintenance.

**Offer item or walk:** offer item with an engineer's letter; walk on
active movement without a documented repair.

## 2. The sewer lateral

Houses built before about 1985 on the Front Range have a sewer lateral of
vitrified clay or, from the 1940s to 1970s, Orangeburg (tar-impregnated
fibre pipe that deforms and collapses). Silver maples, cottonwoods and
elms — every post-war street's trees — send roots into the joints.

**What you see:** nothing. That is the point. A scope ($150–250) shows
roots, bellies, offsets, cracks, or a collapsed Orangeburg section.

**What it costs:** root cutting and a maintenance schedule, $300 a year;
spot repair, $3,000–6,000; full replacement from the house to the main,
$8,000–18,000 in Denver and the older suburbs because the line crosses the
street right-of-way and needs a licensed contractor, a permit and a street
cut. Pueblo and the Springs are somewhat cheaper; Boulder is not.

**Offer item or walk:** offer item, always. A $12,000 replacement is a
$12,000 price reduction or a seller credit, and the line is then the best
one on the block.

## 3. Radon

Colorado is an EPA Zone 1 state; roughly half of homes test above the
4 pCi/L action level, and the foothills and the granite-adjacent metro
west side test highest. The state requires a radon disclosure on every
sale. Tenants increasingly ask.

**What it costs:** a test, $150 (48-hour) during inspection; a mitigation
system — a sealed sump, a suction pipe and a fan — $1,000–2,000. Post-
mitigation levels are usually under 2.

**Offer item or walk:** offer item. Install the system whether or not the
test is close; it is cheap and it is a leasing point.

## 4. The roof

The Front Range from the Wyoming line to Pueblo is one of the hailiest
corridors in the country; the 2017 storm that hit Lakewood, Wheat Ridge and
Golden was Colorado's costliest insured event. Insurers responded: older
roofs are now written at **actual cash value** (depreciated) rather than
replacement cost, wind/hail deductibles have moved from $1,000 flat to
**1–2% of dwelling coverage**, and carriers non-renew on roofs over 15–20
years. Hail, not wildfire, accounts for roughly half of Front Range
premiums.

**What you see:** granule loss, bruising, exposed mat, soft spots; or a
roof that looks fine and is 18 years old.

**What it costs:** $15,000–30,000 for an asphalt roof on a post-war ranch;
10–20% more for Class 4 impact-resistant shingles, which most carriers
discount 10–30% for and which are worth it. Pull the CLUE report for prior
claims — a paid claim with no replacement is a red flag.

**Offer item or walk:** offer item. Get the insurance quote bound before
the inspection objection deadline; if the carrier requires a new roof to
bind, that is your number.

## 5. The electrical panel

Federal Pacific Stab-Lok (1950s–80s) and Zinsco/Sylvania (1960s–70s) panels
have documented breaker-failure problems, and many Colorado insurers will
not bind, or will surcharge, until they are replaced. Denver's and Aurora's
rental-licence inspections and Boulder's flag them. Knob-and-tube in the
pre-1930 belt and aluminium branch wiring in late-1960s–70s houses are the
other two.

**What it costs:** panel replacement $2,500–4,000; aluminium-wiring
remediation with approved connectors $2,000–5,000; knob-and-tube
replacement $8,000–25,000 depending on access.

**Offer item or walk:** offer item; the panel is a half-day job.

## 6. Supply plumbing

Galvanised steel supply lines (pre-1960) corrode closed and reduce pressure;
polybutylene (1978–1995, common in the 1980s subdivisions of Aurora,
Highlands Ranch, Westminster and the Springs' north) fails at the fittings
and many insurers exclude it.

**What it costs:** repipe in PEX, $6,000–15,000 for a ranch.

**Offer item or walk:** offer item.

## The era checklist

| Built | Expect |
| --- | --- |
| 1890–1930 | Clay sewer, knob-and-tube, galvanised supply, boiler, no insulation, brick that needs tuckpointing, a foundation of sandstone or soft brick |
| 1945–1965 | Clay or Orangeburg sewer, FPE panel possible, galvanised or early copper, single-pane aluminium windows, original furnace replaced once, basement that has taken water |
| 1965–1985 | Orangeburg or clay sewer, Zinsco/FPE possible, aluminium wiring (late 60s–70s), polybutylene (late 70s on), expansive-soil movement in the subdivisions that pushed onto the clay |
| 1985–2005 | Polybutylene into the mid-90s, hail-aged roofs, metro districts, the first HOA-era stucco and EIFS problems, radon |
| 2005–2026 | Metro districts, builder-grade roofs on their first hail cycle, and in the 2020–22 cohort, supply-chain substitutions worth a closer look |

## What we always order

Sewer scope. Radon test. A roof inspection separate from the general
inspection on anything over ten years old. A structural engineer's letter
on any foundation crack wider than a quarter inch or any door that sticks.
And the insurance quote, bound, before the objection deadline — because
the insurer's inspector is the one who decides what the roof and the panel
actually cost you. See [the insurance ground notes](/investing/front-range-insurance-hail-wildfire).
