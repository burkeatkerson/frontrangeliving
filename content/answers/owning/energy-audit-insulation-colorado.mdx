The Front Range's housing stock was mostly built between 1950 and 1985,
when natural gas was cheap, insulation codes were thin, and nobody sealed
anything. A 1962 Lakewood ranch with its original attic insulation and a
dozen recessed lights added in 1995 is a chimney: warm air goes up through
the ceiling, cold air comes in at the rim joist and the sliders to replace
it, and the furnace runs all night. The fix is cheap, rebated, and it is not
the windows.

## The audit

A blower door — a fan in the front door that depressurises the house —
measures how leaky it is in air changes per hour; an infrared camera
shows where. The auditor also checks insulation depth, duct leakage,
combustion safety on the furnace and water heater, and produces a
prioritised list with rebate eligibility. What a typical Front Range ranch
shows:

## The windows question

1950s–70s single-pane aluminium sliders are cold to sit near, rattle in a
chinook, and let in dust. They are also only 10–15% of the house's heat
loss, and $15,000–25,000 to replace. After the attic, the honest order is:
weatherstrip and add interior storm panels ($100–300 a window) for the
comfort, then replace for resale and noise when the budget allows, in
fibreglass or wood-clad rather than builder vinyl, which yellows and warps
in our UV. The federal credit covers $600 a year toward ENERGY STAR
windows. On a house you are selling in two years, replace the worst three
on the front and leave the rest; see [what pays back](/answers/improvements-that-pay-back-front-range).

## What you get

A 1960s ranch that goes from R-15 to R-49 with the leaks sealed typically
sees gas use fall 10–25%, the furnace cycle half as often, the back
bedroom stop being the cold one, and the ice dams stop forming. It is also
the prerequisite for a right-sized [heat pump](/answers/heat-pump-colorado-rebates)
and the first thing on [the rebate order](/answers/xcel-time-of-use-rebates-colorado).
