Colorado is a disclosure state with a specific form and a specific
standard: you must tell the buyer what you actually know about material
defects, you need not investigate to find out more, and you may not
conceal. The form is the Colorado Real Estate Commission's Seller's
Property Disclosure; the statutes add radon, lead paint, the metro
district and a few others. On the Front Range, the items that generate
lawsuits are predictable.

## The standard

**Actual knowledge.** You disclose what you know; you are not required
to hire an inspector to find out what you do not. If you commission a
pre-listing inspection — [which we recommend](/answers/prepping-house-for-sale-front-range)
— you now know what it says and must disclose it (and may attach it,
which most sellers do with the repair receipts).

**Material.** A defect a reasonable buyer would consider in deciding
whether to buy or what to pay. A sticking closet door is not; water in the
basement is.

**Repaired counts.** A problem you fixed is still a problem you knew
about: "basement took water at the NE window well in 2023; regraded and
added a cover and a drain; dry since" is the right answer. It reads as a
seller who takes care of the house.

**"Do not know" is an answer.** Use it honestly; do not use it to avoid
"yes."

**No oral disclosures.** If it is not on the form or in writing, it was
not disclosed.

## After closing

Colorado's contract carries the buyer's inspection rights and the SPD's
limitations, but neither protects a seller who concealed a known defect.
The common post-closing claims on the Front Range are misrepresentation
over water intrusion, soils and structural reports, roof claims, and
unpermitted work — all of them avoidable by writing one honest sentence.
The buyer's remedy is damages for the repair; yours, after a concealment
finding, can include their attorney's fees. Disclose the problem, attach
the fix, and sell the house.
