The furnace is the hardest-working appliance in a Front Range house. It
starts in October, runs through April, cycles on for the 20°F mornings in
between, and in a 1960s ranch it is probably the second or third one the
house has had. Taking care of it is mostly a calendar and a filter.

## When it stops

The three 2 am failures, and what to check before you call:

1. **Dirty filter or blocked return** — the furnace runs for a minute and
   shuts off (high-limit trip). Replace the filter, wait 30 minutes for the
   limit to reset.
2. **Flame sensor** — the burners light, then go out after a few seconds,
   three times, then lockout. The sensor is a thin metal rod in the flame
   path; shut off power, pull it, polish it with fine sandpaper or a dollar
   bill, reinstall. Five minutes, saves a $200 call.
3. **Pressure switch / condensate** — the inducer fan runs but the burners
   never light. Check the condensate trap and the intake/exhaust pipes for
   blockage.

If the thermostat is blank, check the furnace's service switch (looks like
a light switch, on the cabinet) and the breaker. If you smell gas, leave
and call Xcel or Colorado Springs Utilities from outside.

Furnaces last 15–20 years here; a cracked heat exchanger at year 18 is a
replacement, not a repair, because the part costs as much as the unit. A
furnace over 20 years old should have a replacement plan and a budget
before October, not after the diagnosis.

## Replacing it: gas or heat pump

Until about 2022 the answer on the Front Range was a 96% gas furnace and a
separate AC. Two things changed. Cold-climate heat pumps (Mitsubishi
Hyper-Heat, Bosch IDS, Carrier Infinity, Daikin Fit and others) now hold
full capacity to 5°F and run to −15°F, which covers all but a few nights a
year at 5,280 feet. And the incentives stacked: Xcel pays $2,250 per ton
for cold-climate units (measured at 5°F), Colorado's state tax credit is
$1,000, the federal 25C credit is 30% up to $2,000, and Denver's CARe,
Boulder County's, Fort Collins's Efficiency Works and Colorado Springs
Utilities' programmes add to it. On a 3-ton system in the metro that is
$7,000–9,250 off a $15,000–20,000 install, for a unit that also replaces
the AC.

Denver's building code restricts new 80% gas furnaces on most replacements
and is moving toward electric-ready requirements; Boulder and Fort Collins
are on similar paths. The practical advice: at end of life, get a dual-fuel
quote (heat pump with the gas furnace kept as backup below 10°F) alongside
the gas-only quote, with the rebates netted out. See [the heat pump and
rebate guide](/answers/heat-pump-colorado-rebates).

## Thermostat and time-of-use

On Xcel's default time-of-use rate the expensive hours are 5–9 pm on
weekdays. With a gas furnace the electricity is only the blower, so the
effect is small; with a heat pump it matters. A smart thermostat set to
pre-heat to 70°F by 4:30 pm and drift to 66°F during peak saves real money
on a heat pump and is rebated $50–100 by Xcel, CSU and Fort Collins. See
[time-of-use and rebates](/answers/xcel-time-of-use-rebates-colorado).
