Every Front Range inspection report on a house built before 1985 has the
same line near the top, and every Front Range homeowner who ignored it has
the same story: the basement floor drain in February, the plumber with the
camera, and the number. The sewer lateral is the most expensive single
component of an older house that nobody can see, and here it has three
things working against it at once.

<Diagram name="sewer-lateral" caption="What you own, and where it fails" />

## Why here

**You own the whole run.** In Denver and nearly every Front Range
municipality, the lateral from the house to the tap at the city main is the
property owner's — including the length under the public sidewalk and the
street. When it fails under the street, you pay for the right-of-way permit,
the traffic control, the cut, and the restoration.

**It was built of the wrong stuff.** Vitrified clay in short sections with
mortar joints (1900s–1950s); Orangeburg tar-impregnated fibre pipe
(1945–1972); early thin-wall ABS in some 1970s subdivisions. Clay joints
separate as the clay soil swells and shrinks; Orangeburg goes oval and
collapses; both let roots in.

**The trees.** Silver maples, cottonwoods, elms and willows — every
post-war parkway tree — send roots toward the only reliable water in a
semi-arid climate, which is the moisture seeping from a sewer joint. A
root mat can fill a 4-inch pipe in three years.

**The soil moves.** Expansive clay lifts and drops the pipe seasonally,
opening joints and creating bellies (sags) where solids settle.

## The scope

A plumber feeds a camera from the cleanout (usually a capped pipe in the
basement floor or just outside the foundation) to the main, recording
distance and depth. What the video shows and what it means:

| Finding | What it means | Typical next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roots at joints, pipe otherwise intact | Normal for clay under trees | Root cutting $250–400; annual foaming root treatment $150–200; rescope in 12 months |
| Belly (standing water) | A sag from settlement | Monitor if small; spot repair ($3,000–6,000) if solids collect |
| Offset joint | Sections shifted by soil movement | Spot repair or lining |
| Cracks, fractured sections | Failing clay | Lining or replacement |
| Oval deformation, delamination | Orangeburg failing | Replace; it cannot be lined reliably |
| Collapse, camera cannot pass | Failed | Replace, now |

Lining — a resin sleeve cured inside the old pipe — avoids the dig and
the street cut and is the right answer for cracked but round clay.
Orangeburg is usually too deformed to line. Trenchless pipe-bursting pulls
a new HDPE line through the old one from two pits and is the middle option
where a contractor offers it.

## Insurance and warranties

A standard homeowners policy does **not** cover the sewer lateral; it
covers the resulting water damage inside only if you carry the **sewer and
drain backup endorsement** ($40–100 a year — carry it). Several Front Range
utilities and cities offer or endorse a lateral-line warranty (Denver Water
and Colorado Springs Utilities have partnered with HomeServe-type
programmes) at $5–15 a month with caps of $5,000–10,000; for a 1950s house
under a big tree, the arithmetic can work. Read the exclusions: most
exclude pre-existing conditions, so buy it *after* a clean scope, not a
bad one.

## The maintenance programme

Scope at purchase; keep the video. Rescope every three to five years, or
annually if roots appear. Cut roots when they show. Never plant a
water-loving tree over the line, and remove the one that is there if the
video says so. And find the cleanout now, before February.
