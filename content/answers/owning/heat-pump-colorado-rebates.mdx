Ten years ago the honest answer was no: a standard heat pump lost half its
output by 20°F and the Front Range's January nights made the backup strips
do the work. The cold-climate generation changed that, and the 2023–26
incentives made it the default recommendation when a furnace or an AC
reaches end of life. The caveats are about sizing and the electric rate,
not whether it works.

Colorado Springs Utilities' programme runs separately (up to about $4,200
for a cold-climate unit in 2026) and stacks with the state and federal
credits; Fort Collins, Longmont and Loveland have their own through
Efficiency Works and Platte River Power Authority. Xcel's amounts are tied

account, because the programme is paid for by gas customers to reduce gas
use.

## What goes wrong

**Oversizing.** Installers used to size for the coldest hour with a margin;
a heat pump sized that way short-cycles all shoulder season, dehumidifies
badly in summer and costs more. Insist on a Manual J load calculation of
the house as it is *after* the attic is insulated, and size for the design
temperature (Denver's is about 0°F) with the backup covering the rest.

**Ducts.** A heat pump moves more air at a lower temperature than a
furnace. 1960s ducts sized for a 140°F furnace and leaking 25% into the
crawlspace make a heat pump feel cold. Seal and, if needed, upsize the
returns; the audit catches it.

**The thermostat.** Heat pumps want a steady setpoint, not the 8°F
night setback a furnace tolerates — a deep morning recovery kicks on the
resistance strips at three times the cost. Set 66–68°F and leave it, with
a small pre-heat before the 5 pm peak on Xcel.

**The panel.** A 3-ton heat pump with 10 kW of backup strips needs a
40–60-amp circuit; many 100-amp panels cannot take it. A 200-amp upgrade
is $2,500–4,500 and 30% of it (to $600) is covered by 25C when done with
the heat pump.

## The code direction

Denver's building code already restricts new 80% gas furnaces on most
replacements and requires electric-ready wiring; Denver's 2027–30 targets,
Boulder's and Fort Collins's climate plans, and Xcel's state-mandated
clean-heat plan all point the same way. None bans a gas furnace
replacement today, and a 96% condensing furnace remains a reasonable
choice on a house with a new AC. But a furnace bought in 2026 will be the
last gas furnace most Front Range houses have, and the rebates are the
state saying so.

## Running it

Service annually like a furnace (outdoor coil cleaned, refrigerant
checked, filter monthly). Keep the outdoor unit clear of snow — it needs
airflow, and the defrost cycle drips, so a pad raised 6–12 inches off the
ground with gravel under it. Expect it to run almost continuously on cold
days; that is how it is meant to work, not a fault. See [the furnace
guide](/answers/furnace-maintenance-colorado) for the rest of the routine
and [the rebate guide](/answers/xcel-time-of-use-rebates-colorado) for the
order of upgrades.
