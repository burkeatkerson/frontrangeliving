faq:
  - q: "Why is there a gap between my basement wall and the ceiling?"
    a: "It is a floating wall, and it is supposed to be there. In expansive-soil areas, basement partition walls are built with a 2–4 inch gap at the top (or bottom) and attached with slip connections, so when the slab heaves the wall can move without pushing up the floor joists above. Covering the gap with trim is fine; nailing the wall rigidly to both slab and joists is not."
  - q: "Which foundation cracks are serious in Colorado?"
    a: "Vertical or slightly diagonal hairline cracks in poured concrete, and stair-step cracks in block that stay the same width, are usually shrinkage or minor settlement and are normal. Get an engineer for: horizontal cracks (soil pressure), cracks wider than a quarter inch, cracks that widen year to year, a wall that bows or leans, cracks with displacement (one side higher than the other), or doors and windows that bind progressively."
  - q: "Should I water my foundation in Colorado?"
    a: "Not on purpose, and not with the sprinklers. The goal is consistent moisture, not wet or dry extremes: keep downspouts discharging five feet out, grade away from the house, and do not run spray heads against the foundation. In a long drought a soaker hose 18 inches from the wall, run briefly, can keep clay from shrinking away from the footing — but more Front Range foundations are damaged by too much water than too little."

Pull up the geology map of the Front Range and the piedmont from Fort
Collins to Pueblo is coloured for the Pierre Shale, the Denver and Dawson
formations and the Laramie — all of them bentonite-bearing clays that take
on water and swell, sometimes by 10% or more, and shrink back when they
dry. The Colorado Geological Survey calls it the state's most costly
geologic hazard. Your house is built on it, and it moves.

## What the clay does

Wet clay swells upward and sideways; dry clay shrinks and pulls away. A
slab poured on it — the basement floor, the garage floor, the patio — rides
up and down with the seasons and with how much water reaches the soil under
it. Foundation walls on footings move less but not zero, and the difference
between the slab's movement and the wall's is where the cracks appear.

What it looks like from inside:

- A basement or garage slab that is higher in the middle than at the
  walls, or that has cracked and lifted along a line.
- Drywall cracks from the corners of doors and windows, especially after a
  wet spring.
- Interior doors that stick in June and swing freely in January.
- A gap opening between the basement floor and the bottom of a partition
  wall — or at the top of the wall, by design (the floating wall).
- Exterior: a patio or walk that slopes toward the house, a driveway
  heaved at the garage, brick veneer cracks at the corners.

A structural engineer — not a foundation-repair company — for $400–800
gives you a letter that says whether the movement is stable, what caused
it, and what if anything to do. Foundation-repair firms sell piers; most
Front Range houses that get a sales visit do not need them.

## The prevention is water

Clay swells because water reaches it. Nearly every Front Range foundation
problem traces to a downspout that dumps at the wall, a patio that slopes
toward the house, a sprinkler zone that soaks the foundation twice a week,
or a window well that fills. See [the drainage guide](/answers/basement-water-grading-colorado)
for the full programme; the essentials:

<Checklist title="Keep the soil steady" items={[
  "Downspout extensions or buried drains carrying water at least 5 ft from the foundation",

  "Window wells with covers and gravel that drains",
  "Gutters clean and not overflowing at the corners",
  "No foundation planting that needs daily water; no trees within 15 ft of the wall",
  "In a deep drought, brief soaker-hose runs 18 in. from the wall to stop the clay pulling away — not flooding",
  "Interior: a sump pump where there is a pit, tested each spring",

Colorado requires a soils report for new construction in expansive-soil
areas and most post-1990 houses in the metro, the Springs and Pueblo West
were built with engineered foundations (drilled piers and grade beams,
structural floors over a void, or post-tensioned slabs) for exactly this
reason. A 1960s ranch was not. That does not mean it is failing; it means
it has had sixty years to settle into its clay, and your job is not to
change the water regime it has gotten used to.

## When you buy or sell

The [inspection ground notes](/investing/what-inspectors-find-front-range)
cover what a buyer's inspector will flag. Colorado's seller disclosure asks
about known foundation and soil problems and any engineering reports — if
you had the letter done, disclose it; a report that says "stable, cosmetic"
is an asset. See [seller disclosures](/answers/colorado-seller-disclosures).
