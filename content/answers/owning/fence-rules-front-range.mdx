HOA covenants are stricter than any city: many Douglas County and
Broomfield communities limit back-yard fences to 4 or 5 feet of open rail
on lots backing open space, and most specify material and stain. Run the
plan through design review before the city.

## The line

Front Range subdivisions from the 1950s–70s were surveyed once and fenced
by whoever got there first; fences a foot or two off the platted line are
common, and the next fence usually follows the old one. Before you set a
post:

The neighbour's fence that has been 18 inches onto your lot since 1985 is
not automatically theirs by adverse possession — Colorado requires 18
years of open, hostile, continuous use and, since 2008, good faith and a
higher standard of proof — but it is a conversation, not a bulldozer.

## Dogs, pools and corners

Pool barriers are a building-code matter in every jurisdiction — 4 feet
minimum, self-closing self-latching gates, permit required. Corner lots
carry a sight triangle (typically 25–30 feet back from the intersection)
where nothing over 3 feet may stand. Front-yard fences above the city's
cap exist all over old Denver and the Springs as legal non-conforming
survivors; replace one and it must meet today's height. And the fence
that the HOA approved in 2004 is the one you may rebuild in kind — a
change of height or style restarts the review.
