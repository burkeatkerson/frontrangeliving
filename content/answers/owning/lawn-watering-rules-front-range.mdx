The Front Range is semi-arid — Denver averages about 15 inches of
precipitation a year, Colorado Springs 16, Pueblo 12 — and a Kentucky
bluegrass lawn wants about 30 inches between May and September. The
difference is your sprinkler system, and every water provider in the
corridor regulates how you use it in summer. 2026 is a drought year in the
Denver system; the rules below are the current ones.

Check your own provider's page in March; the stage can change year to
year. Douglas County's district water is the most expensive on the Front
Range and the most restricted.

## What the lawn needs

Bluegrass at 5,000–6,500 feet, full sun, in July: about 1.5 inches a
week including rain, delivered deep and infrequently so the roots go down.
The Front Range's clay soil absorbs water slowly — a spray zone running
fifteen minutes puts most of the last five on the sidewalk.

## The lawn that needs less

The rules and the rates both point the same way. Turf-type tall fescue
needs about 20% less water than bluegrass and handles foot traffic;
buffalograss and blue grama — the native shortgrass — need a quarter of
the water, green up later and go dormant earlier, and are what Denver
Water and CSU pay you to plant. Colorado's 2024 law bars new non-functional
turf in commercial and HOA common areas and several cities (Castle Rock,
Aurora, Broomfield) cap front-yard turf on new homes. The [xeriscape
guide](/answers/xeriscape-colorado-yard) covers the conversion and the
rebates; the [sprinkler guide](/answers/sprinkler-blowout-colorado) covers
the controller and the backflow test.

## Enforcement

Denver Water and CSU both respond to reports and start with a warning;
repeat violations carry fines ($50–250 in Denver's schedule, escalating)
and, in higher drought stages, flow restrictors. Castle Rock and Aurora
enforce more actively than most. The rule that catches people is the
10 am–6 pm window — a controller left on the seller's noon schedule.
