Stand in Boulder on a January night when the wind comes and you understand
why the houses there have no trampolines. Air piles up on the west side of
the Divide, pours over the top and accelerates down the east slope like
water over a dam, arriving in the foothills towns at 80–100 mph with
gusts that have been clocked at 140. Denver proper and the plains get a
softer version; the corridor as a whole sees 60 mph a dozen times a winter.
Two kinds: the warm, dry **chinook** that takes the temperature from 20°F
to 60°F in an hour and melts the snowpack, and the cold **bora** that
arrives behind a front and does the same damage at 10°F.

## Where it blows

| Zone | Typical winter peak gusts | What to expect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boulder, Louisville, Superior, Gunbarrel | 80–110 mph, occasionally more | The state's windiest populated area; roofs, fences, the Marshall Fire's driver |
| Golden, west Arvada, Ken Caryl, Roxborough, the Hwy 93 corridor | 70–100 mph | Foothills gap winds; Golden's Table Mountains funnel it |
| Denver, Lakewood, Aurora, Littleton | 50–70 mph | Fences, trampolines, shingles on exposed slopes |
| North metro, Brighton, Greeley plains | 50–65 mph, sustained | Steady plains wind; blowing dust and snow; semi rollovers on I-25 and I-76 |
| Fort Collins, Loveland | 60–80 mph | Cache la Poudre and Big Thompson canyon outflows |
| Colorado Springs north, Air Force Academy, Monument | 70–100 mph | Monument Hill closes I-25; the Academy records the city's peak gusts |
| Colorado Springs south, Fountain, Pueblo | 50–70 mph | Southern gap winds; Pueblo's spring dust |

## After a wind event

Walk the lot and look up: lifted or missing shingles (check the yard and
the neighbour's for pieces), ridge caps, the fence line, soffit panels,
the gutters' end caps, tree limbs hung up in the canopy ("widow-makers").
Re-seal lifted tabs with roofing cement on a warm day; replace missing
ones before the next storm, because the exposed underlayment is what leaks.
Wind damage is covered under the wind/hail deductible on most Front Range
policies — which means the $4,500–9,000 percentage deductible applies, so
most wind repairs are out of pocket. See [the insurance guide](/answers/homeowners-insurance-colorado-hail).

## The chinook's other effects

The warm downslope wind is also what makes January 60°F and turns a foot
of snow into an ice dam by the next morning (see [ice dams](/answers/ice-dams-colorado)),
what dries the soil around the foundation in winter (see [winter tree
watering](/answers/trees-front-range-yard)), and — on a day with 5%
humidity and a spark — what drove the Marshall Fire. The wind forecast is
the one weather product worth checking here; the National Weather Service
issues High Wind Warnings for the foothills a day ahead, and that is the
day to bring the umbrella in.
