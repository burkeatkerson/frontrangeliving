The Front Range's 1950s–70s housing stock came with full, unfinished
basements — cheap to pour, and the builder assumed you would finish it
later. Most people do: it is the cheapest square footage you will ever add
to a house here. It is also where three Colorado-specific problems meet —
the clay under the slab, the radon coming through it, and the egress rule
that decides whether the room you build counts as a bedroom.

## The Colorado details the inspector checks

**Floating walls.** Partition walls do not bear on the slab. Either a
2–4 inch gap at the top with the wall standing on the slab and held to the
joists with slip clips, or — more common now — the wall hung from the
joists with a gap at the bottom and the bottom plate pinned through
oversized holes so the slab can move under it. Drywall stops short of the
gap and trim covers it. Bearing walls, if any, go on footings.

**Egress.** Every bedroom — and most inspectors treat any room with a
closet and a door as a bedroom — needs an escape opening: 5.7 square feet
of clear opening, 20 inches wide by 24 inches tall minimum, sill within
44 inches of the floor, a 36-inch-deep well with 9 square feet of floor,
a ladder if the well is over 44 inches deep, and a cover that opens from
inside. Cutting the foundation and setting the header is the structural
part; $3,500–7,000 per window installed, and the single best investment
in the project because it is what turns "finished basement" into "third
bedroom" at sale.

**Fire and life safety.** Smoke alarms in each bedroom and the hall, CO
alarms, interconnected to the rest of the house; a 20-minute door on the
mechanical room; 5/8-inch drywall under stairs and around the furnace in
some jurisdictions; no combustible storage within 3 feet of the furnace.

**Radon.** Seal the slab penetrations and the sump; run a 3-inch passive
vent pipe from under the slab through the roof if one does not exist; add
the fan now if the pre-test was over 4. See [the radon guide](/answers/radon-colorado-home).

**Plumbing.** A bathroom below the sewer line's exit needs an ejector pit
and pump ($2,500–4,500) unless the house has a deep enough lateral; most
Front Range ranches do, which is why so many basement baths were roughed
in at construction. A floor drain near the water heater and the furnace
condensate line to it.

**Insulation and vapour.** Rigid foam against the concrete before the
framing (R-10 continuous, or R-13 batts with the foam as the vapour
barrier) — never poly sheeting against a Front Range foundation wall,
which traps moisture behind it.

## What it is worth

A permitted basement finish that adds a conforming bedroom and a bath
returns roughly 50–70% of its cost in appraised value on the Front Range,
and more in the post-war neighborhoods where the finish creates the third
bedroom and second bath that every buyer's search filter requires. The
appraiser counts below-grade square footage separately and at a lower
rate than above-grade, which is why the egress window and the permit
matter more than the finish level. Unpermitted, without egress, the same
space is "bonus room" and is worth a fraction. See [what pays back](/answers/improvements-that-pay-back-front-range)
and [permits](/answers/permits-front-range-homeowner).
