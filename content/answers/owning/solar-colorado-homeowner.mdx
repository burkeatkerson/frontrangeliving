The Front Range gets about 300 sunny days and some of the strongest
irradiance of any populated area outside the Desert Southwest, the state
has had a solar-rights law since 1979 and net metering since 2005, and the
federal credit is 30% through the early 2030s. Solar pencils here. The
questions are about sequence, the roof, and the rate.

## Does the roof qualify

- **Orientation.** South is best; west is nearly as good on Xcel's
  time-of-use rate because it produces into the late afternoon. East
  works; north does not.
- **Shade.** A big silver maple on the south side costs more output than
  any panel choice recovers. Your installer's shade analysis tells you;
  get two.
- **Age.** Panels last 25–30 years; removing and reinstalling them for a
  re-roof is $2,000–5,000. If the roof is over 10–12 years old, re-roof
  first — ideally with Class 4 shingles for the insurance discount — and
  do both in one season. A roof that has had a hail claim is a roof with a
  clock on it.
- **Structure.** Trusses take it; 1920s rafters sometimes need sistering.
- **Panel.** A 7–10 kW system needs a 40–60-amp backfeed breaker; some
  100-amp panels cannot take it without an upgrade ($2,500–4,500, part of
  the project and partly credited).

## Sizing

Pull twelve months of kWh from your Xcel or CSU account. Xcel caps
net-metered systems at 120% of annual usage; size to 90–100% of it after
the efficiency work is done. A metro house using 8,000 kWh a year wants
about 6–7 kW (15–18 panels) at Front Range production of roughly 1,400–1,500
kWh per kW per year. If a heat pump or an EV is coming, size for the future
load — it is cheaper to add panels now than later.

Payback at those numbers: 8–12 years without a battery, 14–18 with one.
Xcel's pending rate increase shortens it; a future change to net-metering
compensation (Xcel has proposed it, the PUC has so far kept retail
crediting) would lengthen it. Systems installed under the current tariff
are generally grandfathered for a term.

## Time-of-use and the battery

On Xcel's rate, the power you export at noon is credited at the midday
rate and the power you buy at 6 pm costs the peak rate. Three responses,
in order of cost: shift loads into the daytime (laundry, dishwasher, EV
charging, pre-cooling the house at 3 pm), face part of the array west, or
add a battery that charges from the panels and discharges 5–9 pm. The
battery also rides through the wind-storm outages. At $12,000–16,000
installed it is a resilience purchase first and a payback purchase second;
Xcel's battery-connect programmes and the 30% credit soften it.

Colorado Springs Utilities and Fort Collins are not on time-of-use by
default, so a plain south array is the right answer there.

## The HOA, the permit, the utility

Colorado's Solar Rights Act voids any covenant that prohibits solar or
restricts it in a way that cuts output or raises cost more than 10%.
Submit the design to the architectural committee anyway; the statute is
your answer to a denial. Every city permits a solar install (Denver
e-permits; the Pikes Peak Regional Building Department for the Springs);
the installer pulls it and the utility's interconnection agreement. Xcel's
interconnection takes four to eight weeks after inspection; do not sign a
contract whose clock starts before permission to operate.

## At resale

Owned solar appraises — Fannie Mae and the Appraisal Institute's methods
value it at the present value of the electricity it will produce, typically
$10,000–20,000 on a mid-size system — and buyers in Denver, Boulder and Fort
Collins now expect it. A leased system is the opposite: a contract the buyer
must assume, and a common reason deals wobble. See [what pays back when you
sell](/answers/improvements-that-pay-back-front-range).
