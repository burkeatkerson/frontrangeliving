Front Range snow is a different animal from Midwest or Northeast snow: it
falls in bursts, often a foot at a time, and then the sun comes out at
5,280 feet and melts it off the south side by noon while the north side
turns to ice for three weeks. The ordinances are written for that — clear
it fast, before it freezes — and they are enforced on complaint.

## What the sun does for you

At this altitude and latitude, January sun on a south-facing concrete walk
melts an inch of snow in a few hours even at 25°F. Two consequences: the
south and west sides of the house largely clear themselves if you break
the snow up, and the melt runs onto the north-shaded section and freezes
at 4 pm. Shovel the sunny side to speed the melt, and sand the shaded side
where the melt ends up.

## Liability

Colorado's premises-liability statute holds the owner to reasonable care
on their own walkways and, through the ordinance, the public sidewalk. A
slip-and-fall claim runs through your homeowners liability coverage
($500,000 minimum, umbrella above it — see [the insurance guide](/answers/homeowners-insurance-colorado-hail)).
The documentation that defends you is the same as compliance: clear within
24 hours, treat ice, and if you are away, have someone do it. Snow services
run $30–60 per event or $250–400 a season in the metro; many operate with
a 2-inch trigger, so confirm they come for the 1-inch storms that ice over
too.

## Driveways, alleys and the street

Your driveway is yours and unregulated, except that you may not push it
into the street. Alleys in Denver and Colorado Springs are cleared by
adjacent owners or not at all. Residential streets are plowed only after
the main routes; Denver plows residential streets in storms over about 12
inches, and the side streets ice into ruts for weeks — the reason every
Front Range winter ends with a pothole season.
