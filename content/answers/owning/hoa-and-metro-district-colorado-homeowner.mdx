Most Front Range houses built after 1990 come with two layers of governance
that did not exist when the post-war ring was built: a homeowners
association created by recorded covenants, and a metropolitan district
created by the developer to finance the pipes and roads. New owners tend to
conflate them, overestimate what the first can do, and underestimate what
the second costs.

## What the HOA can and cannot do

Colorado's Common Interest Ownership Act (CCIOA) and a run of statutes
since 2019 have narrowed HOA power considerably.

**HB22-1137** (2022) is the one to know. It requires associations to adopt
a written enforcement policy, to notify owners of violations with a cure
period, to contact owners by certified mail *and* one other method before
collection action, to offer a repayment plan of at least 18 months at a
minimum of $25 a month, and it caps fines at the actual cost of correcting
the violation. A board that skips a step loses the ability to collect.

You also have a right under CCIOA to the association's records — budget,
reserve study, insurance declarations, minutes, the enforcement policy — on
request, and to speak at board meetings. Read the budget and the reserve
study once a year; the [condo guide](/investing/condo-vs-townhome-vs-house-investing)
explains why master-policy insurance has been driving dues.

## The metro district

A metropolitan district is not an HOA. It is a special-purpose government,
created under Title 32 by the developer before the first house sold, that
issued bonds to build the subdivision's streets, water and sewer lines,
parks and sometimes the rec centre. The bonds are repaid by a **debt-service
mill levy** on every parcel in the district, typically 30–50 mills, plus an
operations levy, for 30–40 years. It appears on your property tax bill, not
as dues.

Two things the district cannot do: raise its levy above the cap in its
service plan without an election, and foreclose on you directly — unpaid
property tax goes through the county treasurer's tax-lien process like any
other.

Two things it can: issue additional debt up to the authorised amount
(often hundreds of millions across a large community like the Meadows in
Castle Rock or Reunion in Commerce City), and refinance and extend the
bonds. The [property-tax guide](/answers/protest-property-tax-colorado)
has the mill-levy ranges by city.

## When the HOA is wrong

Colorado's Division of Real Estate runs the HOA Information and Resource
Center, which takes complaints and publishes the statutes in plain
language. Associations must register with the Division annually. For a
dispute over a fine or a denied xeriscape plan, the order is: cite the
statute in writing to the board, request a hearing under the enforcement
policy, then the Division's complaint process, then small claims or county
court. Most boards back down at step one when the statute is quoted.
